Kids — the best economics, not the best trend

Kids returns the most revenue on the smallest budget, has the best show-up rate we run at 22.7%, and its CAC-to-value ratio of 6.9% is the best in the portfolio by a wide margin. All of that is true of 2026 as it stands. What is not true is that Kids escaped the year's decline: its headline year-on-year gain rests on a comparison window containing its launch month, and once that is excluded its CAC rose 29% like everything else.

IELTS — steady, but down on last year

The most stable product month to month, and still 22% below its 2025 return. Its 15.9% CAC-to-value ratio looks worse than SEJ's only because the IELTS course sells for BDT 10,738 against SEJ's BDT 27,467. Per taka of course value it still acquires more efficiently than SEJ does.

SEJ — the biggest recoverable pool, and the biggest fall

Highest course value in the portfolio at BDT 27,467, best close rate at 45.3%, worst CAC. At an 8.2% show-up rate, 92 of every 100 registrations evaporate before the class. September posted a CAC of BDT 6,969, the worst month any product has recorded this year. SEJ also carries the year's steepest decline: CAC up 53% and ROAS down a third against 2025.

SES — recovering, but do not push it

CAC eats 27.7% of course value, three times the ratio Kids runs at. But it is improving fastest: CAC down 56% from the first third of the year to the last, and September was its best month at BDT 1,086 and 6× ROAS. Hold it there. SES is the one product where CAC climbs sharply with spend — correlating at +0.80, against +0.13 to +0.31 for the others. Its audience saturates.

PART 2What the data says
The headline

Conversion got better. CAC got worse anyway.

Against the same nine months of 2025, SEJ and IELTS together turned a higher share of leads into students — and still paid 26% more for each one.

Lead-to-admission conversion went from 4.04% to 4.93%, a 22% improvement. On its own that would have pulled CAC down to about 1,355 — comfortably better than last year.

But cost per lead went from BDT 67 to BDT 103, up 54%. That added 895 to CAC. The funnel gave back 461 of it. The net is +434, and a portfolio doing more work for less money.

The funnel team delivered. Media pricing ate the gain and then some.

The scale of it is worth sitting with. Had cost per lead simply held at last year's level, the same spend would have produced roughly 3,612 admissions instead of 2,344 across those two products — about BDT 21M of additional revenue. That single line is larger than every recommendation in Part 3 combined.

Most of that increase has a known cause. From April 2026 these campaigns moved from broad targeting to a 2km radius around the branches, shrinking the addressable audience dramatically. The section on the targeting switch below tests that against the data and sizes what it cost.

Efficiency

Cheap leads are not cheap students

Across 36 product-months, what we pay for a lead tells us nothing about what we pay for a student. The correlation is +0.10 — statistically nothing.

Split the nine months into thirds by lead price and the point is blunt. The cheapest third averaged BDT 58 a lead and produced a CAC of BDT 2,218. The most expensive third averaged BDT 149 — 157% more — and produced BDT 2,634, only 19% worse.

SES proves it from one side: the cheapest leads in the portfolio, and CAC running from BDT 1,086 to BDT 5,640. SEJ proves it from the other: July's leads cost BDT 250, the priciest of the year, and returned a mid-range CAC of 2,333 at 10× ROAS.

A campaign judged on cost per lead can be winning on the scorecard and losing money.

The bottleneck

Eighty-eight in every hundred never arrive

Show-up rate is what sets CAC — it correlates at −0.53, against +0.11 for lead price. Blended across nine months it is 13.3%.

In absolute terms: 90,978 registrations produced 12,105 people in a room. 78,873 registered and never came, and every taka behind them was spent.

We are paying full price for seventy-nine thousand people who never walk through the door.

What makes it worth chasing rather than accepting: the class itself works. 34.6% of people who attend go on to enroll, and SEJ closes 45.3% of them. Whoever gets through the door is the right person. There are simply far too few of them, and the rate is falling.

The gap is widest exactly where it costs most. SEJ sells the highest-value course in the portfolio at BDT 27,467 and converts the fewest registrations into attendance — 8.2%, against Kids' 22.7% on the same funnel. That one rate is the difference between SEJ's CAC of 2,754 and something near 1,700.

Seasonality

March costs us a third of a quarter

Ramadan fell across March in 2025 and from 18 February to 19 March in 2026. In both years attendance collapses while spend and lead flow carry on as normal.

March 2026 against the average of the months either side of it: spend −5%, leads +2%, attendance −53%, admissions −48%. CAC went to 3,183, up 83% and the worst month of the year.

We kept buying at full price into a month when nobody could come. That is roughly 259 admissions and BDT 4.8M of revenue. Had show-up merely held at the February–April norm, March CAC would have been 1,472 rather than 3,183.

One detail confirms the reading: March's close rate went up, to 36.7% from 33.1%. The few who did attend during Ramadan were unusually serious. It is attendance that breaks, not intent. The 2025 data agrees — March was among SEJ's three worst show-up months of that entire year, at 2.3%.

January is a fifth of the year

The opposite pattern sits at the other end. January 2026 produced 16,980 leads and 847 admissions — 20% of all admissions in nine months, in one month — at 12.7× ROAS, and it is the only month attendance cleared 2,900. The Bangladeshi academic year starts in January, which fits. But January 2025 was the launch month for SEJ and IELTS, so it cannot serve as a second reading: this pattern rests on one clean observation and should be treated as a strong hypothesis rather than an established season.

Two months set the year. One we underbuy, the other we overbuy.

What this means for 2027

Ramadan moves about eleven days earlier each year. In 2027 it is expected to run from roughly 7 February to 8 March, with Eid around 8–9 March. The dip we have been absorbing in March will land in February, and the recovery window opens in the second week of March.

Two things follow. Budget planned on a 2026 monthly shape will be pointed at the wrong weeks. And spend should be thinned during the fasting month and loaded into the fortnight after Eid, when intent returns.

Revenue quality

A 31% fall in revenue per student that CAC cannot see

Blended revenue per admission dropped from 19,434 in January to 12,976 in September. No product cut its prices.

Taken product by product, revenue per admission is flat or rising: Kids is level, SEJ is up 22%, SES is level. Only IELTS slipped, by 14%. The entire blended decline is mix.

SEJ has gone from 39% of admissions in January to 8% in September. IELTS has gone from 23% to 48%. We are selling steadily more of a BDT 10,738 course and steadily less of a BDT 27,467 one.

That is defensible as a strategy and alarming as an accident. Nothing in the current reporting would tell you which it is, because CAC and ROAS both look stable while it happens. Revenue per admission belongs on the dashboard next to them.

Media

The auction is not what is holding us back

Within 2026, a bigger budget bought cheaper leads. Across years, leads got 71% more expensive anyway. Both things are true, and the gap between them is the open question.

In all four products, months with more leads had cheaper leads — the correlation between lead volume and lead price runs from −0.25 for SES to −0.59 for Kids. And budget converts to leads almost mechanically, correlating between +0.83 and +0.97.

So within this year there is no diminishing return at the budgets we run — scale is not what is pushing CPL up. Yet cost per lead is 71% above 2025 on the two products we can compare. Something changed between the years that month-to-month volume does not explain, and this sheet holds no campaign, audience or placement detail to test it against.

The one exception stays worth respecting: SES is the only product whose CAC climbs with spend, at +0.80. That is a saturation signal in a narrow audience, not an auction-wide one.

Allocation

Budget is not following the returns

Kids and SES take almost the same share of budget — 19.8% and 19.6%. Kids returns 33.8% of revenue. SES returns 8.4%.

The lead numbers explain how that survives review. SES generates a third of every registration the team produces. It is excellent at the metric most likely to sit on a scorecard and poor at the one that pays salaries. Any volume target written in registrations will keep pulling budget toward the product that converts them worst.

Year on year

Only one product beat last year

Like-for-like against 2025, SEJ and IELTS both bought roughly half as many leads at much higher prices, and both ended up paying more per student. Only Kids improved.

IELTS bought 50% fewer leads at 46% higher cost each. Its close rate improved from 31.5% to 37.6% and its show-up rate held, so lead quality genuinely rose — and CAC still went up 21%, because the price rose further than the quality did. Revenue fell 39%.

SEJ is the steepest fall. Cost per lead up 63%, admissions down 43%, revenue down 41%, CAC up 33%. Its show-up rate actually improved, from 5.8% to 8.2% — but its close rate slipped from 52.7% to 45.3%, and neither was enough against the lead price.

Kids looks like the exception, and mostly is not. Its only 2025 overlap is July–September, and July 2025 was its launch month — revenue per admission of 7,000 against a normal 20,000. Drop that one distorting month and compare August–September alone, and Kids' CAC rose 29% and its ROAS fell 17%. It moved with the others, not against them.

The targeting switch

The 2km radius is what changed — and it is costing more than it returns

Through 2025 these campaigns ran broad: all of Bangladesh, or Dhaka, or Chittagong. From April 2026 they ran inside a 2km radius of the branches. That gives us something close to a natural experiment.

January to March ran broad targeting in both years, so that window shows the underlying year-on-year drift with targeting held constant. April to September ran broad in 2025 and 2km in 2026. The difference between those two windows isolates the switch.

The thesis is right about closing. It is wrong about the price.

The reasoning behind the change — that conversions cluster near the branches — shows up clearly in the data, at exactly one stage. Attendance-to-admission rates jumped after April:

SEJ went from closing 39.8% of attendees to 54.8%. IELTS from 32.8% to 40.3%. SES from 12.2% to 34.2%. People who live near a branch do enroll more readily once they walk in. The premise was sound.

But two things went the other way. Show-up rate fell in all four products after April, and cost per lead rose far more than closing improved. A 2km radius around a handful of branches is a very small audience, and small audiences are expensive to reach repeatedly.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Across April to September, had CAC stayed at its 2025 level for SEJ and IELTS, the same spend would have bought 2,224 admissions instead of 1,408 — 816 more students, worth roughly BDT 12.9M.

What this is and is not

This is the strongest test the data allows, and it points one way. It is not a controlled experiment. Seasonality, creative, budget levels and post-Ramadan recovery all moved in the same window, and the April–September period contains the seasonally weakest months of the year.

One caveat cuts against the conclusion and deserves stating: January to March 2025 was the launch quarter for SEJ and IELTS. Launch months usually carry unusually cheap leads and unusually poor conversion, so the control window's apparent improvement is partly a product maturing rather than targeting doing nothing. The gap is wide enough to survive that, but it is narrower than the table alone suggests.

What to do about it

The answer is probably not reverting to nationwide. It is finding the radius where closing stays strong and the audience is still large enough to buy cheaply — 2km is one point on that curve and we have never tested another.

Run a proper geo holdout: split budget in the same weeks between the current 2km setting and a wider ring — 5km, or district-level around each branch — and compare CPL, show-up and close rate directly. Same period, same creative, same products. That removes every confound this analysis has had to reason around, and it can be read within a month.

Two years

Why ROAS fell, in three numbers

Return on ad spend is arithmetic, not mystery. It is the course price, times how well the funnel converts, divided by what a lead costs. Nothing else goes into it.

Two of the three moved in our favour. Revenue per admission edged up 1.6%. The funnel converted 22% better. Together those would have lifted ROAS by about a quarter.

Cost per lead rose 54%, which on its own cuts ROAS by 35%. That one factor outweighed the other two combined, and ROAS landed at 8.1× against 10.0×.

When cost per lead broke

Through all of 2025, cost per lead never left a band of BDT 58 to 83 — twelve months, no exceptions. Only January 2026 sat inside it. February and March sat just above, during Ramadan, when auction prices in Bangladesh routinely rise. From April 2026 — the month the 2km radius came in — it never returned: 111, 86, 151, 137, 103, 124.

The timing is not subtle. The one month of 2026 that ran broad targeting outside Ramadan, January, cost BDT 78 a lead. The six months on the 2km radius averaged 116.

Most of the revenue fall was a decision, not a failure

Revenue across SEJ and IELTS fell BDT 26.6M, or 40%. That number has been read as a performance collapse. It mostly is not.

Spend on those two products was cut 26%. Had ROAS held at last year's 10.0×, the smaller budget would still have produced about BDT 49.0M rather than 66.0M. So roughly BDT 17.0M of the decline — 64% of it — is simply the result of spending less. The remaining BDT 9.5M, 36%, is the efficiency loss.

That distinction matters for how this gets discussed. If the budget cut was deliberate, two thirds of the revenue drop was bought knowingly. If it was not, the more urgent question is why spend fell 26% while lead volume fell 52% — we did not just buy less, we got much less for what we did buy.

PART 3What to do
Calendar

When to push which product

Each product has its own season, and two of them run opposite to each other. This is the single cheapest lever on the list, because it moves money that is already budgeted.

Three patterns come straight off the grid.

January belongs to Kids and SEJ. Both acquire at 0.55× their own average — the cheapest cell either product has. The academic year starts in January and parents move then.

SES runs backwards. It is the most expensive product in the portfolio in January (2.86×) and the cheapest in September (0.55×). Every taka spent on SES in January is buying at five times the cost of the same taka in September. It is also the one product improving month over month, so the two effects compound.

August and September punish Kids and SEJ. SEJ hits 2.38× and 2.52×; Kids 1.72×. We spent our two largest months of the year, August and September, heavily on the two products that were worst at converting in them.

We are buying Kids in September and SES in January. Both are backwards.

Rebalancing the timing alone — moving 20% of each product's spend out of its three worst months into its three best, with no change to total budget — is worth roughly BDT 5.4M across a nine-month period on current conversion rates.

The assumption this rests on

Every figure above holds each product's CAC constant as its budget moves. That is reasonable for modest shifts — Kids and IELTS show correlations of +0.14 and +0.30 between spend and CAC — but it is an assumption, not a forecast.

If CAC on the products we scale degrades 10%, Option B returns +8.0% revenue instead of +13.0%; at 20%, +3.9%. Admissions go slightly negative in both cases — the gain is in revenue mix, not volume. Move in two steps and re-measure after the first.

SES is cut rather than cancelled deliberately. It is the one product with a genuine saturation signal (+0.80 spend-to-CAC) and the one improving fastest. 14% keeps the recovery alive without funding the saturated end of its audience.

PART 4Caveats

Read before acting

September is a partial monthEvery September figure is month-to-date. SES at BDT 1,086 and SEJ at BDT 6,969 are the best and worst readings of the year — both may be artefacts of what has been logged so far.

No lag adjustmentEach month's spend is divided by admissions recorded in the same month. If a registration takes weeks to convert, CAC is overstated whenever budget is rising.

Monthly CAC is noisyThe within-product spread runs 2.8× to 5.8×. No single month should drive a decision; rolling three-month figures are far more stable.

Media cost onlyExcludes team, agency and the cost of running the free classes. True CAC is higher, and that gap bites hardest on SES.

Correlation is not causeThe show-up link is strong and consistent, but this data shows where to run the experiment, not what it will return.

What this report is built onOne source only: the MoM CAC Analysis sheet as updated on 24 September — spend, registrations, attendance, admissions and revenue, by product and month, for 2026 and 2025. Every figure is computed from those five columns.

The sheet cannot explain the show-up gapIt records that 88% of registrations never attend. It holds nothing about why — no reminder, scheduling, timing or capacity data — so this report sizes that gap and stops there rather than guessing at a cause.

The forecasts are models, not predictionsEvery figure in Part 3 holds current CAC, close rates and revenue per admission constant while budget moves. That is a reasonable first approximation and a poor second one. The sensitivity case beside Option B is the number to argue about.

Each cell of the calendar is one yearThe heatmap rests on a single observation per product-month. January and March repeat across 2025 and 2026 and are safe to plan on; the mid-year pattern is one year of evidence and should be treated as a hypothesis to test.

Ramadan dates are approximate2027 dates depend on moon sighting and may shift by a day. The eleven-day annual drift does not.

2025 is a ramp year, not a steady stateSEJ and IELTS launched in January 2025, Kids' data begins in July 2025 and SES's in December. Every year-on-year comparison here measures 2026 against a year in which the products were still finding their footing, which generally flatters 2026 on conversion and flatters 2025 on lead cost.

Comparison depth variesSEJ and IELTS have nine 2025 months each, Kids has three (one of them its launch month), SES has none in the overlap. The portfolio year-on-year figures use SEJ and IELTS only.

The targeting test is observationalSeasonality, creative, budget and the post-Ramadan recovery all moved alongside the April switch. The difference-in-differences controls for what it can; only a live geo holdout settles it.

One reconciliation gapThe Kids September row is still excluded from the lead, attendance and admission totals, and from the Total row's spend — a range that stops one row early. Figures here are computed from the rows, not the total lines.
